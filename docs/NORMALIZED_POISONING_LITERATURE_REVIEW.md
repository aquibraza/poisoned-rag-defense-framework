## Project motivation from the repository diagnostics

PoisonedRAG established that corpus poisoning can drive attacker-chosen responses in retrieval-augmented generation by injecting a small number of malicious passages into a large corpus, and it explicitly found that then-available defenses were insufficient. That makes post-retrieval defenses such as RAGDefender and FilterRAG important, but it also means their assumptions should be stress-tested under controlled, local benchmark conditions rather than taken as stable properties of poisoned retrieval sets. citeturn12search0turn12academia36

Your local repository evidence gives a precise reason to test similarity-based defenses under embedding normalization. The baseline that produced the existing RAGDefender results is a self-contained port in `defense/defense_runner.py`, not a runtime import of the upstream `ragdefender` package. In the HotpotQA multi-hop path, Stage 1 estimates an adversarial count from passage-passage cosine statistics using an OR rule over mean and median thresholds plus a flip branch, and Stage 2 selects the top `C(N_adv,2)` most similar non-self pairs and scores passages by accumulated signed `sim^2` pair frequency. The baseline uses `paraphrase-MiniLM-L6-v2`, and your diagnostic reimplementation was written specifically to expose the same intermediate quantities without changing the baseline behavior. fileciteturn2file1 fileciteturn3file6 fileciteturn3file12

Those diagnostics already isolate the geometry that matters. In the representative success case, all Stage-2 top pairs are poison-poison, the poisoned passages form a complete dense clique, and RAGDefender removes exactly the five poisoned passages. In the over-removal case, high poison-clean similarity inflates `N_adv` and forces spillover into poison-clean and clean-clean edges. In the residual-poison case, one poisoned passage survives because the last-selected and first-excluded Stage-2 scores differ by only `0.0002`, so deterministic insertion-order tie-breaking decides which poison remains. In the severe failure case, the retrieved clean passages form the denser cluster: `mean_clean_clean_similarity = 0.992` exceeds `mean_poison_poison_similarity = 0.945`, all three Stage-2 top pairs are clean-clean, three clean passages are removed, and all five poisoned passages remain. The visualization script itself states that Stage-1 threshold margins and Stage-2 frequency-score gaps are continuous quantities that could support a future normalized adaptive objective. fileciteturn2file0 fileciteturn3file7 fileciteturn3file3

That is enough to justify the central research question: if a defense works because poisoned passages occupy the densest cosine neighborhood, what happens when poisoned passage embeddings are transformed so their pairwise similarity profile looks statistically more like clean-clean or poison-clean structure, while retrieval membership and answer influence are held fixed as much as possible? The literature below supports that question in three distinct senses: feature distributions can be deliberately aligned, clustering outputs can be manipulated without conspicuous outliers, and nearest-neighbor or embedding-based downstream decisions are known to be sensitive to small geometric changes. fileciteturn2file0 fileciteturn3file3 citeturn0search0turn1search5turn3academia38turn17search0

## Distribution matching and representation normalization

**Baochen Sun, Jiashi Feng, and Kate Saenko. 2016. _Return of Frustratingly Easy Domain Adaptation_. AAAI. DOI: 10.1609/aaai.v30i1.10306.** CORAL is the cleanest formal precedent for your proposed oracle stress test. It applies a linear transform that aligns the second-order statistics of a source feature distribution to a target one. In your setting, poisoned embeddings can be treated as the source distribution and clean retrieved embeddings as the target distribution. That directly supports an oracle intervention that asks whether RAGDefender still works after the poisoned set is covariance-aligned to the clean set. The limitation is equally important: CORAL matches covariance, not full neighborhood structure, so it may leave local top-pair orderings unchanged unless additional pairwise constraints are imposed. citeturn0search0

**Baochen Sun and Kate Saenko. 2016. _Deep CORAL: Correlation Alignment for Deep Domain Adaptation_. ECCV Workshops 2016. arXiv:1607.01719.** Deep CORAL extends the same idea to nonlinear representations by adding a differentiable covariance-matching loss inside a deep model. This matters for your framing because it shows that “make one representation cloud look like another” is not an ad hoc security trick but a standard feature-alignment objective in representation learning. For a controlled RAG defense evaluation, it motivates a learned or parameterized embedding-space map that reduces poison-poison concentration while staying close to the original geometry. Its limitation is that it presumes direct access to features or the encoder training loop. That fits an oracle stress test, but it does not by itself show text-space realizability. citeturn14academia29turn0search6

**Mingsheng Long, Yue Cao, Jianmin Wang, and Michael I. Jordan. 2015. _Learning Transferable Features with Deep Adaptation Networks_. ICML 2015. PMLR 37:97–105.** **Arthur Gretton, Karsten M. Borgwardt, Malte J. Rasch, Bernhard Schölkopf, and Alexander Smola. 2012. _A Kernel Two-Sample Test_. JMLR 13:723–773.** DAN operationalizes maximum mean discrepancy by matching source and target hidden representations in an RKHS, while Gretton et al. provide the underlying MMD statistic. For your problem, MMD is stronger than covariance alignment because it can push the poisoned set to match the clean distribution under a kernel-defined notion of distributional similarity, not just second moments. That makes it a natural candidate for a “pairwise similarity distribution matching” oracle. The limitation is practical: MMD alignment can reduce a global distribution gap while still leaving a few extreme poison-poison pairs intact, which may be enough for a top-pair-density defense to fire. citeturn1search5turn1search0

**Bohan Li, Hao Zhou, Junxian He, Mingxuan Wang, Yiming Yang, and Lei Li. 2020. _On the Sentence Embeddings from Pre-trained Language Models_. EMNLP 2020. DOI: 10.18653/v1/2020.emnlp-main.733.** This paper is especially relevant because it is about sentence embeddings, not vision features. Li et al. show that off-the-shelf BERT sentence embeddings are anisotropic and propose BERT-flow, which maps them toward a smooth isotropic Gaussian with normalizing flows. That is almost exactly the representation-normalization mechanism your hypothesis targets: a defense built on cosine concentration may depend on anisotropy and directional crowding in embedding space. If isotropization changes neighbor structure and similarity concentration without changing sentence identity, then a similarity-based defense can be fragile to purely geometric normalization. The limitation is architectural mismatch: BERT-flow studies BERT-family sentence embeddings, while your exact baseline uses `paraphrase-MiniLM-L6-v2`, so transfer has to be argued empirically rather than assumed. citeturn2search0turn3file16

**Junjie Huang, Duyu Tang, Wanjun Zhong, Shuai Lu, Linjun Shou, Ming Gong, Daxin Jiang, and Nan Duan. 2021. _WhiteningBERT: An Easy Unsupervised Sentence Embedding Approach_. Findings of EMNLP 2021. ACL Anthology ID: 2021.findings-emnlp.23.** WhiteningBERT shows that a very simple whitening-based post-processing step can substantially change sentence embedding behavior and improve semantic similarity performance. For your review, the key point is not the benchmark gain. It is that low-cost post-processing can materially reshape cosine geometry in sentence embedding space. That supports a family of oracle stress tests where poisoned embeddings are whitened, sphered, or normalized before RAGDefender sees them. The limitation is that whitening is a global transform. If retrieval rank preservation is critical, a naive whitening step may move both poisoned and clean passages and could confound the interpretation unless only the poison subset is transformed in the oracle setting. citeturn0search5

**Jiaqi Mu and Pramod Viswanath. 2018. _All-but-the-Top: Simple and Effective Postprocessing for Word Representations_. ICLR 2018.** **Tianyu Gao, Xingcheng Yao, and Danqi Chen. 2021. _SimCSE: Simple Contrastive Learning of Sentence Embeddings_. EMNLP 2021. DOI: 10.18653/v1/2021.emnlp-main.552.** Mu and Viswanath show that removing the mean vector and dominant principal directions changes representation quality, while SimCSE argues theoretically and empirically that contrastive learning regularizes anisotropic sentence spaces toward greater uniformity. Together, they support the narrower claim you need: cosine neighborhoods are not invariant facts of a text set. They depend on post-processing and training-induced geometry. That is exactly why a clustering defense that assumes poisoned passages must remain unusually concentrated deserves a normalization stress test. The limitation is scope. These works show sensitivity of representation geometry, but they do not target adversarial clustering defenses, so the bridge to RAGDefender remains a reasoned extrapolation rather than a direct experimental result. citeturn1search1turn2search1

**Ji Xin, Chenyan Xiong, Ashwin Srinivasan, Ankita Sharma, Damien Jose, and Paul N. Bennett. 2022. _Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval with Momentum Adversarial Domain Invariant Representations_. Findings of ACL 2022. DOI: 10.18653/v1/2022.findings-acl.316.** MoDIR is useful because it moves the discussion from general representation learning into dense retrieval. The paper starts from the observation that dense retrieval depends on locality properties of the embedding space and then uses adversarial domain-invariant training to reduce domain-specific structure. This supports a retrieval-specific version of your stress test: ask whether a poison set can be made less domain-distinct, less internally concentrated, or more mixed with surrounding clean neighborhoods while still remaining retrievable. The limitation is that MoDIR is a defense for transfer learning in dense retrieval, not a poisoning method, so it supports feasibility of representation alignment rather than providing an attack template. citeturn6search5

Taken together, CORAL, MMD-based adaptation, isotropy/whitening work, and retrieval-specific domain-invariant learning provide strong literature support for the claim that embedding distributions can be deliberately reshaped to resemble a target distribution. That is the exact methodological foundation of a cluster-normalized oracle stress test. What this literature does **not** prove is that a matched embedding distribution is reachable by natural-language rewriting under a frozen sentence encoder. That second step needs separate evidence from text attack and paraphrase work. citeturn0search0turn1search5turn2search0turn0search5turn2search1turn6search5

## Clustering and density robustness under adaptive perturbation

**Anshuman Chhabra, Abhishek Roy, and Prasant Mohapatra. 2020. _Suspicion-Free Adversarial Attacks on Clustering Algorithms_. AAAI 2020. arXiv:1911.07015.** This paper shows that clustering can be manipulated by perturbing a single sample near a decision boundary such that multiple unperturbed samples become misclustered. The authors emphasize that the perturbed sample need not become an obvious outlier and that the exact clustering metric need not be known. For your setting, that is an unusually strong conceptual match: a similarity-based RAG defense can fail even if no individual poisoned passage looks extreme, because the attack objective can operate through how local pairwise structure alters cluster assignments and spillover behavior. The limitation is that the paper studies centroid-style clustering rather than sentence-embedding top-pair graphs, so the transfer is conceptual rather than one-to-one. citeturn4academia22

**Antonio Emanuele Cinà, Alessandro Torcinovich, and Marcello Pelillo. 2022. _A black-box adversarial attack for poisoning clustering_. Pattern Recognition 122:108306. DOI: 10.1016/j.patcog.2021.108306.** Cinà et al. formulate poisoning of clustering as a constrained black-box optimization problem and show transferability across clustering models. This is directly relevant to your evaluation framing because it validates the idea of a black-box or score-only stress test against a clustering-based defense. If a clustering objective can be degraded without gradients or full model access, then a defense whose removal logic depends on dense pair graphs and cluster concentration should be tested under similar black-box distribution matching objectives. The limitation is again domain mismatch: the paper targets clustering quality broadly, not retrieval retention or target-answer influence, so your constraints would need to be added explicitly. citeturn3search8

**Francesco Villani, Dario Lazzaro, Antonio Emanuele Cinà, Matteo Dell’Amico, Battista Biggio, and Fabio Roli. 2026. _Sonic: Fast and transferable data poisoning on clustering algorithms_. Information Sciences 738:123140. DOI: 10.1016/j.ins.2026.123140.** Sonic is important because it explicitly targets graph-based and density-based clustering methods such as HDBSCAN and studies transferability. Your concern about RAGDefender is not simple k-means fragility. It is fragility of an algorithm that first uses concentration and then uses a dense top-pair subgraph. Sonic shows that poisoning against density-sensitive clustering is feasible at scale and can transfer across algorithms. That makes “graph/density clustering robustness” a legitimate source of stress-test ideas for a RAG defense built on dense pair structure. The limitation is that Sonic manipulates clustering datasets directly and does not impose semantic or retrieval-preservation constraints, so it supports the vulnerability class, not the full RAG-specific objective. citeturn3search0

This clustering literature supports a narrow but useful conclusion for your paper: if a defense relies on poison passages forming the densest region, then an adaptive evaluation should test whether that density advantage can be diluted, redistributed, or shifted toward mixed or clean neighborhoods without creating obvious outliers. Your own severe case, where clean-clean pairs dominate Stage 2, is a concrete local example of a clustering failure mode that this broader literature says should be expected and actively stress-tested. fileciteturn3file7 citeturn4academia22turn3search8turn3search0

## Embedding-space and nearest-neighbor manipulation

**Yizhen Wang, Somesh Jha, and Kamalika Chaudhuri. 2018. _Analyzing the Robustness of Nearest Neighbors to Adversarial Examples_. ICML 2018 workshop paper. arXiv:1706.03922.** Wang et al. show that robustness properties of nearest-neighbor methods depend strongly on \(k\), and that small-\(k\) nearest-neighbor decisions can be inherently non-robust. This matters for RAGDefender because its Stage-2 decision rule is dominated by a small set of top similar pairs, which is a more brittle statistic than full-distribution reasoning. The paper does not study RAG, but it does support the general principle that local nearest-neighbor structure can be unstable under adversarial perturbation. citeturn13academia36

**Chawin Sitawarin, Evgenios M. Kornaropoulos, Dawn Song, and David Wagner. 2021. _Adversarial Examples for k-Nearest Neighbor Classifiers Based on Higher-Order Voronoi Diagrams_. NeurIPS 2021.** This paper provides a geometric attack view of k-nearest-neighbor models by searching over higher-order Voronoi cells to find low-norm adversarial perturbations. It is relevant because it formalizes the idea that a point can move across nearest-neighbor decision boundaries with carefully chosen, small geometric changes. In your benchmark, the analogous question is whether poisoned passages can be moved just enough in embedding space to change top-pair composition from PP-dominated to mixed or CC-dominated while still staying in the retrieved set. The limitation is that the paper assumes continuous perturbability of inputs and a classifier setting, whereas your oracle transform acts on retrieved passage embeddings and must preserve rank and generation influence. citeturn13search3

**Yihan Wu, Hongyang Zhang, and Heng Huang. 2022. _RetrievalGuard: Provably Robust 1-Nearest Neighbor Image Retrieval_. ICML 2022. PMLR 162:24266–24279.** RetrievalGuard is valuable even though it studies image retrieval. The core message is that 1-NN retrieval is vulnerable enough that a specialized robustness mechanism and certificate are needed to keep Recall@1 invariant under perturbation. That supports your methodological premise: retrieval and nearest-neighbor decisions are not passive byproducts of an encoder. They are attack surfaces. For a similarity-based RAG defense, this means one cannot assume cosine neighborhoods are stable under adversarial or normalization-style transformations. The limitation is modality. The result is not a text passage study, so it should be cited as retrieval geometry evidence, not as direct proof about sentence encoders. citeturn5search5

**Aleksandar Bojchevski and Stephan Günnemann. 2019. _Adversarial Attacks on Node Embeddings via Graph Poisoning_. ICML 2019. PMLR 97:695–704.** **Hengtong Zhang, Tianhang Zheng, Jing Gao, Chenglin Miao, Lu Su, Yaliang Li, and Kui Ren. 2019. _Data Poisoning Attack against Knowledge Graph Embedding_. IJCAI 2019. DOI: 10.24963/ijcai.2019/674.** These papers are not retrieval papers, but they matter because they show that embedding-based downstream behavior can be manipulated by poisoning the relational structure that generates the embeddings. For your problem, they support the causal link between altered embedding geometry and altered downstream decisions. If graph edges or facts can reshape node and relation embeddings enough to change classification or link prediction, then there is nothing unusual about asking whether passage embedding geometry can be reshaped enough to change a clustering defense’s removal decisions. The limitation is again scope: these works poison the data structure that produces embeddings, not a set of already-retrieved passage embeddings. citeturn7search7turn7search2

**Yongkang Li, Panagiotis Eustratiadis, Simon Lupart, and Evangelos Kanoulas. 2025. _Unsupervised Corpus Poisoning Attacks in Continuous Space for Dense Retrieval_. SIGIR 2025. DOI: 10.1145/3726302.3730110.** This is one of the closest analogues to your proposed stress test. Li et al. explicitly argue that dense retrieval happens in continuous embedding space and therefore optimize in embedding space directly, with an objective that preserves geometric closeness while maximizing textual dissimilarity. For your literature review, the critical point is that the community already accepts continuous-space optimization as a meaningful way to study dense retrieval poisoning. Your proposed oracle test is narrower and more defensive: instead of asking how to maximize retrieval damage, it asks whether a similarity-based defense breaks when poison embeddings are normalized to look less self-concentrated. The limitation is that Li et al. optimize for corpus poisoning success, not for defense evasion under fixed top-\(k\) retrieval. citeturn17search0turn17search1

**Jinyan Su, Preslav Nakov, and Claire Cardie. 2024. _Corpus Poisoning via Approximate Greedy Gradient Descent_. arXiv:2406.05087.** AGGD is relevant because it shows that corpus poisoning quality depends on how token-level perturbations are searched to affect dense retrievers, and that better structured search improves success and transfer. That supports the broader claim that embedding-sensitive downstream behavior can be manipulated by optimization objectives targeted at retrieval geometry. In your paper, AGGD is best used as a bridge argument: if an oracle normalization stress test reveals a fragile zone, later text-space approximation can use structured search rather than random paraphrase to try to realize it. The limitation is that AGGD is still a text-generation attack paper, not a distribution-matching paper, so it belongs in the “bridge to realizability” stage, not in the oracle stage. citeturn6academia35

This body of work justifies two concrete claims. First, nearest-neighbor and retrieval decisions are geometrically fragile enough to merit explicit robustness analysis. Second, continuous embedding-space objectives are already standard in dense retrieval attack research. Those claims are sufficient to support an oracle experiment that smooths or redistributes poison-poison similarity and then asks whether RAGDefender’s top-pair graph still identifies the poisoned set. citeturn13academia36turn13search3turn5search5turn17search0turn6academia35

If an oracle embedding intervention identifies the kind of similarity redistribution that defeats RAGDefender, TextFooler-like constrained substitution is one plausible route for testing partial text-space approximation. ## Cluster-Normalized Poisoning framework

A defensible title for the methods section is **“Cluster-Normalized Poisoning: An Oracle Stress Test for Similarity-Based RAG Defenses.”** The motivation comes directly from your local diagnostics. RAGDefender succeeds when poisoned passages occupy the densest Stage-2 top-pair subgraph, over-removes when poison-clean bridges inflate `N_adv`, partially fails when near-tied Stage-2 scores leave one poison behind, and catastrophically fails when clean passages form the denser subgraph and consume all top pairs. Those are exactly the signatures one would expect if the defense depends on relative concentration in embedding space rather than on poison semantics per se. fileciteturn2file0 fileciteturn3file7

A useful formal objective is the following. Let \(q\) be a query, \(R_k(q)\) the retrieved top-\(k\) passages, \(P \subset R_k(q)\) the poisoned passages, and \(E(\cdot)\) the frozen passage encoder used by the defense. Define transformed poison embeddings \(z'_p = T_\theta(z_p)\) for \(p \in P\), with clean embeddings held fixed. Choose \(T_\theta\) to reduce poison-poison concentration and poison-dominated top-pair density, while keeping three constraints as stable as possible: the same passages remain in the retrieved set, their rank order changes minimally, and the target-answer influence of the poisoned set under the generator remains unchanged or nearly unchanged. In this form, the experiment is explicitly an **embedding-space oracle stress test**, not a claim about realizable text attacks. That distinction matches the function of CORAL, MMD matching, isotropy normalization, and continuous-space dense retrieval optimization in the literature. citeturn0search0turn1search5turn2search0turn17search0

The core metrics should mirror the quantities already exposed by your diagnostics and visualization code: mean poison-poison cosine similarity, mean poison-clean cosine similarity, mean clean-clean cosine similarity, max poison-poison similarity, top-pair composition as PP/PC/CC counts, nearest-neighbor poison ratio, neighborhood entropy, RAGDefender’s `N_adv` estimate, Stage-2 frequency scores, `removed_poison`, `removed_clean`, residual poison fraction, and downstream ASR. These metrics are well aligned with the current script outputs, which already compute graph-level similarity summaries, per-passage frequency scores, and removal outcomes. fileciteturn3file10 fileciteturn2file0

The candidate interventions can then be interpreted cleanly. **Pairwise similarity distribution matching** tests the narrowest hypothesis: can PP similarities be pushed toward the PC or CC distribution while preserving retrieval and target influence? **CORAL-style covariance alignment** tests whether second-order geometry alone explains the defense’s success. **MMD-style distribution matching** tests higher-order distribution alignment beyond covariance. **Whitening or isotropy normalization** tests whether the defense is fragile to generic representation de-crowding or dominant-direction removal. **Text-space paraphrase or diversification approximation** tests whether an observed oracle weakness is even partly reachable with natural language. Each intervention proves something different. Oracle embedding transforms prove geometric fragility of the defense. They do **not** prove attack realizability in text. Text-space approximations prove partial realizability only if the rewritten passages are re-encoded, re-retrieved, and still preserve generation influence. citeturn0search0turn1search5turn2search0turn0search5turn16search0turn8search5

A sensible experimental sequence is short and controlled. Start from one query where RAGDefender currently works because the poison clique saturates the Stage-2 top-pair graph. Apply one oracle normalization at a time to the poisoned embeddings only. After each step, recompute the full cosine matrix, Stage-1 `N_adv`, Stage-2 top pairs, and removal decisions. The key diagnostic question is whether the top-pair graph shifts from poison-dominated to mixed or clean-dominated before the poisoned passages lose retrieval membership or generation influence. Only after an oracle failure is found should you move to text-space search, using constrained paraphrase or diversification to approximate the same geometric shift. That procedure is directly responsive to the near-threshold and clean-density behaviors your diagnostics already surfaced. fileciteturn3file3 fileciteturn2file0

The most important warning is interpretive. **Embedding-space oracle stress tests are not real-world attack claims.** They show that a defense relies on a geometric assumption that can fail under controlled representation changes. **Text-space adaptive passage mutation is a second, harder question.** It asks whether fluent rewrites can realize the same geometric weakening. **Real-world attack claims require more than either of those.** They require a concrete passage-generation procedure, preserved retrieval into the victim pipeline, preserved target-answer influence, and robust results across queries and retrievers. Your paper should maintain those boundaries explicitly. That discipline will strengthen, not weaken, the result. A clean oracle failure is already a meaningful negative result about the limits of similarity-based defenses. citeturn17search0turn16search0turn8search5

Finally, later comparisons should include defenses that do **not** depend on the same geometric assumption. Your repository’s FilterRAG baseline is a per-passage statistical filter that scores each document independently, rather than inferring adversariality from cross-passage concentration and top-pair density. That makes it a useful later control when you test whether a failure is specific to similarity-based clustering or reflects a broader poisoning difficulty. ML-FilterRAG is an even stronger later comparison once training infrastructure is added. Those comparisons belong after the oracle study, because the present question is specifically whether RAGDefender-style cosine-density assumptions are fragile to normalization or distribution matching. fileciteturn2file2 citeturn12academia36
